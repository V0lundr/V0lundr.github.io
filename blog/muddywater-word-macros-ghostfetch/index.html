    MuddyWater Weaponizes Word Macros in Targeted Campaign Delivering GhostFetch

    MuddyWater Weaponises Word Macros in Targeted Campaign Delivering GhostFetch¶

    In February 2026, I identified a new phishing campaign associated with MuddyWater, framed as a travel reservation from Air Arabia. When recipients enable the document's embedded macro, it installs and executes the GhostFetch loader. This post analyses the campaign's lure design, delivery mechanism, and GhostFetch behaviour, and provides practical guidance for defenders.

    Background¶

    MuddyWater (also tracked as UNC5667, Earth Vetala, Mango Sandstorm, MUDDYCOAST, Seedworm, and TA450) is an Iranian state-aligned APT group operating on behalf of Iran's Ministry of Intelligence and Security (MOIS). Active since at least 2017, the group primarily targets government, telecommunications, energy, and financial sector organisations across the Middle East, North Africa, and South Asia.1 MuddyWater consistently relies on macro-enabled Office documents as initial access vectors, combined with staged payload reconstruction and business-themed lures tailored to regional targets.

    GhostFetch is a first-stage downloader attributed to MuddyWater, publicly documented by Group-IB as part of Operation Olalampo — a campaign first observed on 26 January 2026 targeting organisations across the MENA region.2 GhostFetch performs environmental evasion checks before fetching and reflectively loading secondary payloads in memory, ultimately delivering GhostBackDoor — a second-stage implant that provides interactive shell access, file manipulation, and AES-encrypted C2 communications through French-named API endpoints.3 The analysis here was conducted independently and predates Group-IB's public reporting, allowing for cross-referencing against their findings on the delivered GhostBackDoor implant.

    Reservation Document Lure Analysis¶

    I identified a malicious Word document associated with MuddyWater uploaded to VirusTotal from the UAE on 30 January 2026. The file, titled "Sajeev Saliha Beevi.doc," masquerades as a legitimate Air Arabia reservation itinerary. The document embeds detailed booking information, including the reservation number, flight routes between Abu Dhabi (AUH) and Thiruvananthapuram (TRV), passenger details, and travel agent information. Its professional formatting and realistic travel metadata indicate that the lure was crafted to appear operationally authentic.

    Although the original delivery mechanism remains unknown, the content suggests MuddyWater aimed this document at individuals or organisations with ties to the UAE travel corridor — including corporate travel coordinators, finance departments, and business travelers.

    The document's metadata further supports attribution to MuddyWater and aligns with the group's established operational practices. The file lists "DontAsk" as the author and "FireToRain" as the last saving user, with Microsoft Office Word as the originating application. The document contains 10 recorded revisions with an approximate total editing time of six minutes. The "Content created" timestamp of 25 January 2026 at 12:52 AM and "Date last saved" timestamp of 26 January 2026 at 11:55 AM indicate a compressed preparation window of roughly 24–72 hours before deployment.

    This pattern is consistent with MuddyWater operational workflows observed across multiple campaigns. Earlier reporting identified lure documents authored on Windows systems and saved under operator-associated usernames including "jojo" and "phoenix". Taken together, the metadata artefacts, lure construction, and macro-based execution workflow support a moderate to high confidence assessment that the document reflects established MuddyWater tradecraft.

    While the document appears benign to the end user, technical analysis reveals embedded VBA macros configured to execute automatically when the file opens. The macro functions as a first-stage dropper — it reconstructs an encoded Windows PE file embedded within a "UserForm" object, writes the payload to disk, and executes it.

    VBA Macro Analysis¶

    The macro is designed to execute when the document opens in Microsoft Word with macros permitted — either through explicit user consent or in environments where macro security controls are disabled or insufficiently enforced.

    Once triggered, the macro initiates a staged execution sequence that reconstructs and deploys a concealed second-stage payload embedded within the document. The malicious logic resides entirely within the VBA project. When opened in the Word VBA editor, the VBA project appears password protected.

    However, extraction of the VBA streams confirms that the primary execution logic resides within the "ThisDocument" class module.

    The macro implements a staged workflow. It first calls a delay function (wait()), likely to impede automated analysis. It then retrieves a numerically encoded string from UserForm1.TextBox1.Text and decodes it in three-digit increments to reconstruct a Windows Portable Executable (PE) payload.

    If the payload does not already exist on disk, the macro writes the decoded content to C:\Users\Public\Documents\MicrosoftWordUser.log, renames it to MicrosoftWordUser.exe, and executes it via ShellExecute. This sequence functions as a self-contained dropper that reconstructs and launches the embedded second-stage payload.

    The numerically encoded string retrieved from UserForm1.TextBox1.Text serves as the embedded payload container. The associated UserForm1/o stream contains a large sequence of decimal values.

    The macro's decoding routine processes this string in three-character increments, converting each three-digit value into its corresponding ASCII byte to reconstruct the raw binary payload in memory.

    Before writing and executing the reconstructed executable, the macro checks for the presence of MicrosoftWordUser.exe using the Dir() function, preventing redundant deployment. It writes the payload as MicrosoftWordUser.log, renames it to MicrosoftWordUser.exe, then instantiates the Shell.Application COM object and invokes ShellExecute to launch the renamed executable.

    In summary: when Document_Open() triggers, the macro introduces artificial delays, decodes the numerically encoded payload from a UserForm stream, writes it to C:\Users\Public\Documents\MicrosoftWordUser.log, renames it to MicrosoftWordUser.exe, and executes it via Shell.Application.

    GhostFetch Analysis¶

    The executable dropped by the VBA macro is GhostFetch — the first-stage downloader documented by Group-IB as part of Operation Olalampo.2 Unlike the macro, which functions solely as a delivery mechanism, GhostFetch implements a multi-stage execution flow covering environmental evasion, persistence installation, and encrypted C2 communication. Static analysis identifies it as a 64-bit Windows PE compiled using Microsoft Visual C++.

    Anti-Analysis / Anti-Debugging / VM Detection¶

    Before any persistence or network activity begins, GhostFetch runs an anti-debugging check at FUN_140006080. It first inspects the Process Environment Block (PEB), checking the BeingDebugged flag and NtGlobalFlag. If either indicates a debugger is present, GhostFetch immediately exits.

    GhostFetch performs a screen resolution check at FUN_140004190 as an additional sandbox and VM detection layer. The function calls GetMonitorInfoW to retrieve display dimensions, then compares them against a hardcoded whitelist of width and height combinations. Valid widths are 1440, 1920, and 2560 pixels; valid heights are 900, 1080, 1200, and 1600 pixels. If the detected resolution does not match any expected combination, GhostFetch sets an abort flag — sandbox environments and VMs commonly run at atypical resolutions such as 800×600 or 1024×768.

    GhostFetch also implements a composite sandbox detection routine at FUN_140004260, invoked only if the screen resolution check has already flagged the environment as suspicious. Rather than relying on a single indicator, the function combines three independent hardware checks and returns 1 to trigger abort only if all three conditions are simultaneously true — minimising false positives on real endpoints.

    The three checks are: logical processor count (fewer than two CPUs flags as sandbox), total physical RAM (less than 2 GB flags as sandbox), and USB device history via the Windows registry (fewer than two historically connected devices flags as sandbox).

    GhostFetch enumerates all running processes and compares them against a hardcoded list of security tools and analysis frameworks. If any listed process is detected, execution aborts immediately. The enumeration operates in two phases: a targeted check for antivirus product processes, followed by runtime decoding of obfuscated analysis tool names.

    The complete list of targeted processes covers every major category of analysis tooling — debuggers (ollydbg.exe, immunitydebugger.exe, windbg.exe, x32dbg.exe, x64dbg.exe), disassemblers (ida.exe, ida64.exe, idaq.exe, ida664.exe, pestudio.exe), dynamic instrumentation (frida-helper-32.exe, frida-helper-64.exe), process inspection tools (processhacker.exe, procexp.exe, procmon.exe), network analysis tools (wireshark.exe, fiddler.exe, httpdebugger.exe), and sandbox platforms (joeboxcontrol.exe, joeboxserver.exe).

    Mouse Logging¶

    GhostFetch implements a system-wide mouse logging capability at FUN_1400040c0. The function installs a low-level mouse hook using SetWindowsHookExW with hook type 0xE (WH_MOUSE_LL), intercepting all mouse events system-wide — clicks, movements, and scroll wheel input — before they reach any application.

    Persistence¶

    After successful execution, GhostFetch installs a persistent copy of itself at %LOCALAPPDATA%\Microsoft\Windows\BurnUtill\burn.exe. Both the directory name and subdirectory structure are reconstructed at runtime from obfuscated short arrays decoded through a per-position subtraction scheme. The self-copy logic first decodes the target directory path, creates it if absent, opens the running executable for reading, copies the PE image into a heap-allocated buffer, and writes the result to the decoded destination path.

    C2 Infrastructure¶

    The analysed sample maintains a hardcoded list of 20 C2 hosts. The primary entry, promoverse[.]org, is stored in plaintext and embedded directly in the binary as raw character bytes with no obfuscation.

    The full C2 request URL hxxps://promoverse[.]org/api/debut is assembled at runtime from three components stored separately in the binary. The host appears as raw character bytes; the URL path /api/debut is stored as an encoded short integer array decoded using the same XOR-based decoder used for other obfuscated strings. All three components are passed to WinHttpOpenRequest at runtime.

    The remaining 19 C2 entries are lightly encoded — each stored as a byte sequence recovered at runtime by adding a fixed numeric offset. In the analysed sample, all 19 decode to invalid placeholder strings following the pattern 999.999.999.999x, counting down from 9998 to 9980. This is inconsistent with real operator infrastructure and strongly suggests the binary was compiled with generated placeholder values. The slots can hold up to 16-character strings, and the presence of promoverse[.]org in slot zero confirms the mechanism can store domain names.

    As this analysis was being finalised, Group-IB published their Operation Olalampo report independently documenting the same loader and including a retrieval of the encrypted second-stage payload from promoverse[.]org.2 This timing allowed cross-referencing their findings on the delivered GhostBackDoor implant against static analysis, filling the one gap that could not be resolved from the binary alone.

    According to Group-IB, GhostBackDoor functions as a second-stage implant that adapts its persistence mechanism based on available system privileges — often masquerading as the Windows Recycle Bin ClassID or establishing itself as a service named MicrosoftVersionUpdater. Once reflectively loaded into memory, the backdoor facilitates interactive shell access and file manipulation through AES-encrypted communications with French-named API endpoints, purposefully fragmenting network traffic into separate requests to evade behavioural detection.3

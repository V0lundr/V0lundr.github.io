    An Invoice Lure That Installs a Rogue ManageEngine Endpoint Central Agent

    Summary

    In June 2026 I analysed a multi-stage VBScript intrusion set distributed under a financial-document ("bill" / "invoice") lure. Rather than dropping bespoke malware, the chain disables User Account Control (UAC) and then silently installs a genuine, unmodified, vendor-signed ManageEngine Endpoint Central (UEMS) agent that is pre-configured to enrol with an attacker-controlled management server. This post walks through the lure, the layered VBScript droppers, the UAC-weakening helper, the weaponised agent bundle and the command-and-control configuration.

    Key Takeaways

    • I assess with high confidence that this is a deliberate abuse of a legitimate Remote Monitoring and Management (RMM) product (ManageEngine Endpoint Central) as a backdoor. The installed payload is the real, Zoho-signed agent.
    • The malice lives entirely in the delivery chain, the enrolment configuration and a pair of attacker-generated root certificates.
    • The initial-access vector is a .vbs file sent under a Malay-language "please check your bill" lure (Sila semak bil anda..vbs).
    • The chain first disables the UAC consent prompt (ConsentPromptBehaviorAdmin = 0), then runs msiexec /qn to enrol the agent against the operator's own Endpoint Central server at 38.55.151[.]63:8383, granting the attacker full remote command execution, software deployment and file transfer over a trusted, signed channel.

    Initial-Access Vector Analysis

    Lure Design and Targeting

    The campaign presents at least two complementary social-engineering surfaces. The first observed is a web/email lure styled as an official electronic-invoice notification. It announces an "April 2026" e-invoice, supplies a "bottle password" (112299) needed to open the attached PDF, and offers a prominent Download invoice (PDF) button: the familiar pattern of a credential- or payload-delivery page dressed up as routine finance correspondence (Figure 1).

    The second is delivery over WhatsApp. A chat thread shows a contact pushing a file named Sila semak bil anda..vbs (Malay for "Please check your bill"). I found this campaign while pivoting off breadcrumbs from the "Electronic Invoice" lure campaign (Figure 2).

    I assess that the WhatsApp .vbs and the e-invoice page are two arms of the same operation: both lean on a financial-document pretext, and VirusTotal links the recovered first-stage script and its sibling lures to a single staging domain (below).

    Its workflow is as follows:

    • It inflates itself to ~65 kB with thousands of junk "TRUSTED_INSTALLER", "SECURE_BOOT" and "WINDOWS_DEFENDER_AV_SCAN_COMPLETED" strings, plus tens of thousands of padding characters, to defeat size-based heuristics and look like a large, benign update file.
    • It creates a hidden working directory C:\Users\Public\Documents\MSUpdate_<random>\ and copies living-off-the-land binaries into it under DLL-looking names: curl.exe becomes winhttp.dll and bitsadmin.exe becomes wininet.dll.
    • It fetches hxxps://baskwms[.]top/ksf/uapdf.pdf, renames it to 989.vbs and executes it (the UAC-neutralising helper, below).
    • After a 40-second sleep it fetches hxxps://baskwms[.]top/lingf/lfsag.vbs and executes it (the payload fetcher).

    The download routine is deliberately resilient, cycling through curl, then bitsadmin, then certutil until a file of plausible size lands on disk. The .dll aliases for curl/bitsadmin are a masquerade intended to frustrate process-name-based detection.

    ManageEngine Agent Abuse Analysis

    The content of ponsk.zip downloaded by lfsag.vbs is where the operation reveals itself. The archive is not malware in the conventional sense, but a ManageEngine Endpoint Central LAN-agent deployment bundle.

    File Role
    UEMSAgent.msi (33 MB) Genuine ManageEngine UEMS - Agent v6.0.3, Manufacturer Zoho Corporation Pvt. Ltd., ProductCode {6AD2231F-FF48-4D59-AC26-405AFAE23DB7}. VirusTotal 0/56, unmodified and legitimate.
    UEMSAgent.mst A near-empty Windows Installer transform (_SummaryInformation, _ForceCodepage only), a no-op transform required by the install command line rather than by MSI itself. VirusTotal 0/56.

    UNC5342 Blockchain Dead-Drop Loader

    Summary

    While tracking DPRK-nexus weaponised package activity, I pivoted from a Socket Threat Research report published on 31 May 2026 describing a UNC5342 (Famous Chollima) JavaScript loader appended to the development branch of a legitimate Packagist-listed PHP package.1 Using the loader's distinctive in-file obfuscation stub as a VirusTotal Intelligence content selector, I identified a further sample sharing the same builder, injected into the codebase of an AI SaaS platform for padel in Spain.

    The loader is nearly identical across the observed samples: a JavaScript stage appended to an otherwise-legitimate file behind a large whitespace gap, executing within the Node.js runtime and resolving its second-stage payload from cryptocurrency blockchain infrastructure (TRON, Aptos, and BNB Smart Chain RPC services) used as a dead-drop resolver rather than a conventional command-and-control domain. Only a per-build campaign marker and the host file vary between samples (the obfuscation framework, decoder seed, and resolver design are constant).

    It is highly likely that the affected application is a legitimately-built commercial product compromised through injection rather than an actor-authored artefact, and as a realistic possibility that the compromised codebase is itself being repurposed as Contagious Interview lure material for subsequent developer victims. Family attribution to the UNC5342 cluster is assessed with high confidence on the basis of a shared loader builder and delivery tradecraft consistent with Socket's reporting.

    Background

    UNC5342 is a DPRK-nexus threat cluster that has targeted software developers since at least 2023 through fraudulent technical-interview and recruitment schemes, an activity set widely tracked as Contagious Interview. The cluster is reported under several overlapping vendor designations (including Famous Chollima, DeceptiveDevelopment, and Void Dokkaebi), and its operations are characterised by the distribution of malware through trojanised packages across multiple open-source ecosystems (npm, PyPI, Go, crates.io, and, more recently, Packagist), GitHub repositories, and coding-task assignments presented as part of a hiring process. Targeting has historically concentrated on developers in the cryptocurrency, blockchain, and financial-technology sectors, with the operational objective of harvesting developer secrets, source-control credentials, cloud and CI/CD tokens, and cryptocurrency wallet material from compromised workstations.

    On 31 May 2026, Socket Threat Research published an analysis of a UNC5342 loader injected into the development branch dev-drewroberts/feature/test-case of the legitimate Packagist-listed PHP package roberts/leads.1 Socket assessed the activity as a likely repository or branch compromise, a poisoned-branch workflow consistent with a Contagious Interview lure, rather than a package authored as malicious from the outset; the package's stable release line did not exhibit the indicators. The malicious code was appended to the tailwind.js configuration file behind a large whitespace gap and, once deobfuscated, behaved as a JavaScript loader that resolved its payload from blockchain infrastructure (TRON and Aptos transaction data as payload pointers, BNB Smart Chain RPC transaction input as encrypted payload material), decrypted it with hardcoded XOR keys, executed it via eval(), and spawned a hidden detached node -e second stage. Public reporting attributes the downstream payloads of this loader to the InvisibleFerret and BeaverTail families.23

    This note documents a pivot from that report. The in-file artefacts Socket described, principally the loader's obfuscation framework and decoder seed, provided durable, content-searchable selectors that proved to be shared across multiple samples and host files, enabling the identification of further victims beyond the originally reported PHP package.

    Pivot Methodology

    The pivot proceeded from content, not infrastructure. Socket's report did not publish a conventional command-and-control domain to track. The loader resolves its payload from blockchain RPC services rather than a fixed host, so the durable selectors were the loader's own in-file artefacts. Critically, Socket itself published these artefacts: its "Suggested local check" section provided a grep command intended for analysts to scan their own developer machines, listing the loader's distinctive strings, and its "Deobfuscation Findings" and "Indicators of Compromise" sections documented the same values.1 Among the strings Socket published were the obfuscated decoder variable _$_1e42, the decoder seed rmcej%otb%, and the campaign marker global['!'] (observed as 9-0264-2, expanding to A9-0264-2).

    The initial selector for finding additional samples was the decoder stub variable _$_1e42, an unusual token unlikely to collide with legitimate code:

    content:"_$_1e42"

    This returned files beyond Socket's original roberts/leads sample. Examination confirmed the hits shared the full obfuscation construction, not merely the variable name: the same decoder function fed the seed rmcej%otb% with the multiplier 2857687, and the same global[_$_1e42[0]]=require reconstruction. The seed string was used as a corroborating second selector, being even less prone to coincidental collision:

    content:"rmcej%otb%"

    The observed sample contained an injection into an application source file of the affected application; despite arriving in a different host-file type like in the Socket research, it carried an identical decoder stub. The differences were limited to the campaign marker (9-0264-2 in Socket's case, 9-33 here) and the host file into which the loader was appended. The decoder framework, the rmcej%otb% seed, the multiplier, and the blockchain-resolver design were the same.

    Loader Family Analysis

    Naming and Prior Reporting

    The loader analysed here has been documented publicly under several names, reflecting different vendors and different scopes of the same activity. Google's Mandiant and Threat Intelligence Group, relayed by the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security, track the blockchain dead-drop technique as EtherHiding and the specific multi-stage downloader as jadesnow.42 OpenSourceMalware track the obfuscator and an associated mass GitHub-injection campaign as PolinRider, documenting the identical decoder signature (rmcej%otb%, multiplier 2857687, variable _$_1e42) and the same blockchain wallets and XOR keys recovered in this analysis.3 Ransom-ISAC and Crystal Intelligence describe the same chain as Cross-Chain TxDataHiding.5 Public reporting attributes the downstream payload to the InvisibleFerret and BeaverTail families.23

    Obfuscation Structure

    The loader analysis showed that the appended stub was built from four nested decoding layers, each feeding the next:

    1. A first decoder, assigned to the variable _$_1e42, performs a seeded Fisher-Yates-style shuffle of the seed string rmcej%otb% with the multiplier 2857687, followed by a character-substitution pass. It yields a small string table; the loader immediately uses it to reconstruct global['r'] = require, masking the require reference from naive static scanners.
    2. A second decoder, sfL, of the same shuffle design but different constants, expands two large encoded blobs: the first into a third decoder, and the second into the loader body.
    3. The third decoder is a dictionary or word-substitution decompressor that expands the loader body into readable JavaScript bearing a single remaining string table, _$_ccfc.
    4. A final decoder resolves _$_ccfc into the operational strings, the API paths, method names, cryptocurrency addresses, and XOR keys, on which the loader depends.

    The salient point for hunting is that the campaign marker (global['!'], here 9-33) is the only element that meaningfully rotates between builds, alongside the host file. The decoder construction, the seed rmcej%otb%, and the multiplier are invariant across all examined samples, and are therefore the reliable family selectors. This is corroborated by OpenSourceMalware, who independently documented the same four-layer structure and the same ('rmcej%otb%', 2857687) / _$_1e42 signature across their campaign dataset.3

    Payload Resolution: a Blockchain Dead-drop

    Once decoded, the loader is an asynchronous function with no hardcoded command-and-control domain. Instead it resolves its payload through public cryptocurrency blockchain infrastructure used as a dead-drop, a design that places the controlling pointer and the payload body on immutable, high-availability public ledgers that are impractical to take down and that blend with legitimate blockchain traffic.

    The resolution proceeds in two stages, each independently parameterised with its own wallet, account, and decryption key:

    • The loader first queries the TRON network via api.trongrid.io, retrieving the most recent outbound transaction for a hardcoded wallet and extracting a pointer from its raw_data field (hex-decoded, reversed, and reassembled). Should this fail, it falls back to the Aptos network via fullnode.mainnet.aptoslabs.com, reading the pointer from a hardcoded account's latest transaction payload.
    • Using that pointer, the loader issues a JSON-RPC eth_getTransactionByHash request to BNB Smart Chain (bsc-dataseed.binance.org, falling back to bsc-rpc.publicnode.com) and reads the transaction's input field as the encrypted payload body. The body is then XOR-decrypted with a hardcoded repeating key.

    Execution: Two Stages and a Hidden Child Process

    The loader runs two payloads in sequence, each resolved through the mechanism above with a distinct wallet and key:

    • The first payload is executed in-process via eval().
    • The second is executed by spawning a detached child process: node -e <payload>, with detached: true, stdio: 'ignore', and windowsHide: true, so the second stage runs as an independent, windowless background process that survives the parent and produces no visible console. The campaign marker is propagated into the child through global['_V'], so the second stage inherits the build tag. An eval() of the same payload is retained as a fallback should the spawn fail.

    A thirty-second self-throttle (global['_p_t'], compared against Date.getTime()) prevents the loader re-running on rapid re-require, reducing duplicate execution and associated noise.

    The second-stage payloads themselves were not recovered. They do not reside in the file; they are retrieved at runtime from the blockchain, the payload pointer from a TRON transaction (Aptos as fallback) and the encrypted payload body from the referenced BNB Smart Chain transaction. Recovery therefore requires an on-chain retrieval of the relevant transactions followed by XOR decryption with the recovered keys, and is recorded as an outstanding task.

    Relationship to the Socket-reported Sample

    Deobfuscation of the 9-33 sample resolved the operational string table to indicators that are identical to those Socket published for the roberts/leads case: the same two TRON wallets, the same two Aptos accounts, the same two XOR keys, and the same RPC hosts.1 The samples are therefore assessed with high confidence to originate from the same builder and to share the same staging infrastructure, not merely the same technique. They are not byte-identical: the campaign marker differs (9-33 against 9-0264-2), the host file differs (the application route module AccountDataRoutes.js against tailwind.js), and the per-build encoded inner strings differ.

    The Affected Application

    The host of the 9-33 sample appears to be a real commercial product: a multi-tenant SaaS platform for padel and tennis club management, built for the Spanish market by a third-party software vendor and marketed as an AI-assisted assistant for clubs. It's worth noting that the identifying details are masked throughout.

    The platform looks like a substantial build, not a thin shell: a Node.js, Express, and MySQL backend that integrates with established court-management platforms (Playtomic, Syltek, Taykus) as a complementary automation layer, with booking automation, Stripe subscription billing, WhatsApp Business messaging, MQTT venue and lighting control, and an energy-management integration. It is publicly documented as an associate member of an international padel industry body since February 2025, with a public website and social-media presence.

    Injection Footprint and Deployment Path

    Across the entire codebase, the loader was present in exactly one file: AccountDataRoutes.js, the Express route module handling account and Stripe billing data. Every other component, the services, controllers, middleware, scheduled jobs, utilities, the application entry point app.js, and the dependency manifests, was clean. The malicious JavaScript was appended after the file's legitimate module.exports = router; behind a large whitespace gap, the same parasitic-append pattern Socket documented for the tailwind.js case. The route is mounted in app.js, so the loader executes whenever the application loads that module.

    Five-Stage PowerShell Loader Analysis

    The macro leaves two artefacts behind, neither of them directly executable. The first is the file C:\Users\Public\Microsoft\default.ps1, whose every line begins with Error: and which, to a casual look, resembles a corrupted log file. The second is the in-memory PowerShell process that cmd.exe spawns, parameterised by the base64-encoded script body inside the $rukp variable on its command line. The file on disk is ciphertext. The script on the command line is the decryptor. The two are designed to fit together only at execution time.

    To recover the malicious behaviour, I peeled away the wrappers in the order the malware itself would peel them. Between the base64 sitting on cmd.exe's command line and the final PowerShell HTTP RAT that beacons to the C2, there are five distinct unwrap operations. Each layer uses one of two primitives - a base64 decode, or an XOR against a mixed key - and each layer's output is the next layer's input:

    • Stage 1: outer cmdline base64

    • stage-2 PowerShell with Invoke-EnvironmentCheck + scheduled-task action

    • Stage 2: UTF-16LE -Enc base64 (the task's Argument)

    • $rukp = '<base64>'; iex wrapper

    • Stage 3: inner ASCII base64

    • decryptor: reads default.ps1, strips "Error:", base64+XOR, iex

    • Stage 4: default.ps1 XOR-decrypted with key:salt = E8/LNvsiT2w/IyTlSVyqPQ==:aYcZU4lHrxI=

    • mixed key 7a48d2657265e07e56a43db6c01b052f

    • yet another $rukp = '<base64>'; iex wrapper

    • Stage 5: inner base64

    • final HTTP RAT

Inside the Invoke-Request function, and again inside the runspace initialiser that backs the implant's parallel uploader, the script defines a Get-Cookie routine that writes a JScript file named aes_<guid>.js into the user's %TEMP% directory and executes it with cscript.exe //nologo. The file is built at request time from two pieces fetched over the network: the slowAES JavaScript library served at /aes.js on the C2 host, and the HTML challenge page served at the C2 root. The implant strips the HTML wrapper and the location.href redirect tail from the page, substitutes WScript.Echo(toHex(slowAES.decrypt(c, 2, a, b))) for the redirect logic so that cscript prints the cookie value to standard output instead of navigating away, concatenates the two pieces into aes_<guid>.js, runs the file through cscript, and captures the resulting 32-character hex string. The string becomes the test cookie that the implant attaches to every subsequent MODE-headered POST, and a finally block removes the temporary file once cscript exits.

    The reason this helper exists is that the C2 is hosted on InfinityFree, the free hosting platform behind the 42web[.]io domain, and InfinityFree fronts every customer site with a JavaScript anti-scraping check that requires the browser to solve a slowAES challenge before any further requests are accepted. Without a valid __test cookie, the C2 would reject the implant's POSTs as bot traffic, so the helper is a precondition for every C2 round trip rather than a stage in its own right. It does not appear in the five-layer unwrap above for the same reason: it does not wrap the next layer, it has no encoded body of its own, and its JavaScript content lives on the C2 rather than inside any of the stages on disk.

    Two design choices here are worth calling out. First, the task name is MicrosoftEdgeUpdateTaskMachine-<user SID>. Legitimate Microsoft Edge update tasks use a {GUID} suffix. Second, the loader registers the task twice: once in the user's own context, and then again under SYSTEM at -RunLevel Highest. The second call is wrapped in a try { ... } catch { } swallow, so on an unprivileged victim it silently fails and only the first registration sticks. On an admin or UAC-bypassed session, both succeed and the task runs as SYSTEM thereafter. That is the only privilege-escalation surface in the chain - there is no exploit, just an opportunistic attempt that costs nothing if it fails.

    The third design detail is the choice of conhost.exe as the Execute argument with powershell.exe as its Argument. That trampoline forces conhost.exe to spawn powershell.exe as its child, which complicates simple parent-process detections that key on Office spawning PowerShell directly. The parent of the recurring PowerShell is therefore conhost, not cmd and not winword, and only the initial detonation produces the WINWORD -> cmd -> powershell tree.

    Detection and Mitigation

    I recommend the following layered approach to prevention, detection, and hunting:

    Prevention and Hardening

    • Macro Restrictions: Block macro execution in documents originating from outside the organisation. Verify that Microsoft's Mark-of-the-Web (MotW) macro-blocking GPOs are strictly enforced.

    • PowerShell Constraints: Restrict PowerShell to Constrained Language Mode (CLM) for non-administrative users and verify that AMSI providers remain attached and functional.

    Host-Based Detection

    • Malicious Execution Chains: Alert on a WINWORD.EXE ancestor spawning a cmd.exe → powershell.exe chain where the command line contains -NoP, -exec BYpass, -w hIdden, and a base64 argument exceeding three kilobytes in length.

    • Suspicious Scheduled Tasks (Naming): Alert on the creation of scheduled tasks named MicrosoftEdgeUpdateTaskMachine-S-1-5-*. The legitimate Edge updater uses a {GUID} suffix; the presence of a user SID is a high-fidelity anomaly.

    • Suspicious Scheduled Tasks (Execution): Alert on scheduled-task actions where the Execute target is conhost.exe and the Argument starts with powershell.exe. This trampoline technique is highly irregular for native Windows scheduled tasks.

    • Masqueraded File Drops: Alert on the creation of any file under C:\Users\Public\ whose first line matches the regex ^[A-Za-z]+:\s+[A-Za-z0-9+/]{40,}={0,2}$. The Error: <base64> masquerade is distinctive, and benign software does not produce this pattern.

    Network-Based Detection

    • Custom HTTP Headers: Hunt and alert on outbound HTTPS POST traffic carrying a MODE request header with the values LI, RP, GC, UF, or DF. This is not a standard HTTP convention and should yield a near-zero noise hunt.

    • C2 Infrastructure: Block egress traffic to updatepowershellservice[.]42web[.]io. Consider blocking the wider *.42web[.]io namespace if business requirements permit.

    Threat Hunting

    • Mutex Sweeps: Hunt for the Global\SysInfoProject_* mutex using EDR telemetry (specifically querying for CreateMutex API calls) or by conducting live sweeps across the fleet using tools like handle.exe.

Before taking any action, the loader enumerates running processes and compares them against a hardcoded list of analysis, virtualisation, and forensic tooling, and it exits silently if any match is found. The presence of Sysmon alone was sufficient to trigger this check.

    In the second phase the sandbox was re-provisioned with the Sysmon service and VirtualBox helper processes removed, so the sample would proceed past its environment check. This run produced the complete attack chain, including scheduled task persistence, a second execution stage, and command and control traffic. Elastic Defend retained full process, file, network, and registry visibility throughout both phases because its sensor does not run under any of the process names the sample screens for. The alerts and detection gaps described below are drawn from this second, fully executed run unless stated otherwise.

    Across the fully executed run, Elastic Defend generated alerts from ten distinct detection rules. These fired at each major stage of the attack, from the initial document execution through to the command-and-control (C2) beacon.     The genuinely significant behaviours, namely the scheduled task persistence and the command-and-control activity, surfaced only as Low and Medium alerts. This fragmentation, combined with the inverted severity, is the central weakness this exercise exposed, and it directly motivates the new and tuned detections proposed in the next subsection.

    Detection Gaps and Recommended Rules

    While the existing rule set produced alerts at most stages of the chain, several of the most security-relevant behaviours generated no alert at all. These gaps matter because they represent the techniques an analyst would most want flagged, and because they are the steps least likely to be benign in a production environment. The following behaviours were observed in the telemetry but did not trigger any detection rule.

    The first gap is the use of cscript.exe to execute a JScript file from a user-writable temporary directory. On every C2 round trip the implant's Get-Cookie helper wrote a JScript file (aes_<guid>.js) into the user's %TEMP% directory and executed it through the Windows Script Host (cscript.exe //nologo), which breaks the PowerShell process lineage and continues execution under a different interpreter. No rule covered a script interpreter launching a script from a temporary or public path, despite this being a reliable indicator of staged execution.

    The second gap is the use of conhost.exe as a proxy launcher. The scheduled task action invoked conhost.exe with powershell.exe and an encoded command as its arguments, using the console host as an intermediary to launch the payload. While an unusual parent-child rule did fire on the resulting relationship, no rule specifically identified conhost.exe being supplied another executable as an argument, which is an anomalous and high-confidence signal.

    The third gap is the scheduled task masquerade. Persistence was registered under a task named to impersonate a legitimate Microsoft Edge update task, while its action pointed to an encoded PowerShell command rather than the genuine updater binary. The generic scheduled task creation rule did fire, but only at Low severity and without regard to the deceptive naming or the mismatch between the task name and its action. A detection that keys on this name and action mismatch would be both higher fidelity and higher severity.

    The fourth gap is the malicious document writing a script to disk. The macro dropped a PowerShell script into a world-readable location under the Public user profile before execution began. No rule flagged an Office application writing a script or executable file type, even though Office processes have no legitimate reason to author such files in normal use.

    The fifth gap is the sandbox and security tooling discovery performed by the loader. Before proceeding, the script enumerated running processes and compared them against an extensive list of virtualisation, debugging, and forensic tools. This is the behaviour that suppressed the first detonation entirely, and it is among the most distinctive signatures of this sample, yet nothing detected it. Capturing this reliably depends on PowerShell script block logging, which is addressed as a visibility gap below.

    The sixth gap is the command-and-control (C2) beacon to free hosting infrastructure. The second stage resolved and connected to a domain on a free web hosting provider, and the repeated connections to the same destination indicated beaconing behaviour. The existing remote file download rule fired on the associated activity, but there was no detection keyed on a script interpreter contacting free or anomalous hosting infrastructure, and no analytic identifying the periodic beacon pattern itself.

    Underlying several of these gaps is a visibility limitation rather than a missing rule. Elastic Defend observed only the outer command lines of the PowerShell stages, so the decrypted script bodies, the environment check logic, and the decryption routine were never available to the detection engine. Enabling PowerShell script block logging and ingesting it into Elastic SIEM is the single highest-value improvement available here, because it would expose the deobfuscated script content that rules five and the reflection-based loader behaviour depend on. This should be treated as a prerequisite for the script-content detections rather than an optional enhancement.

    Taken together, these gaps share a common theme. The existing rules detect well-known atomic techniques such as Office spawning a shell or PowerShell using encoded arguments, but they do not cover the evasion, masquerade, and staging behaviours that distinguish this sample from commodity activity. The recommended rules below are designed to close those gaps, and to favour the behaviours that are both high fidelity and difficult for the actor to alter without changing their tradecraft.
